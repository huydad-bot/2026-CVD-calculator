2Quick-Start Guide (3 minutes)
  1. In Basic Info & Labs, enter age, sex, total cholesterol, HDL-C, LDL-C, triglycerides, systolic BP (and whether on BP treatment), diabetes, smoking and eGFR.
  2. If the patient is already on a statin, check Currently on a statin and enter the statin intensity and on-treatment lipids — pre-treatment values are back-calculated automatically.
  3. Review the recommended intensity, LDL target and decision basis in Statin Prescribing Recommendation on the right.
  4. Press Copy in Result Summary for EMR Copy and paste it into the medical record.
3Detailed Guide (step by step)
  1. Basic Info & Labs — for a postprandial sample, check Non-fasting (the TG threshold changes from fasting ≥150 to non-fasting ≥175). If eGFR is left blank, 90 is used.
  2. Additional inputs (UACR · HbA1c · height/weight) — UACR and HbA1c each trigger the corresponding PREVENT add-on model even when entered alone (Full model if both are entered). UACR changes the risk substantially, so always check the unit (mg/g). BMI affects heart failure risk only.
  3. Currently on a statin — pre-treatment lipids are back-calculated from the average response for the selected statin intensity. The risk category is based on the pre-treatment baseline; on-treatment risk is shown alongside for reference. The result summary reflects whether the current target is met.
  4. Special conditions (Detailed mode) — HIV (stable cART), confirmed HeFH (clinical or genetic), statin intolerance (muscle symptoms on 2 or more statins, one at the lowest approved dose — 2026 Figure 18)
  5. Risk enhancers — triglycerides, albuminuria, CKD, ABI and others are checked automatically from the inputs. Diabetes-specific risk enhancers (Table 17: duration, albuminuria, eGFR<60, retinopathy, neuropathy, ABI<0.9) are enabled only when diabetes is checked in Basic Info.
  6. Subclinical ASCVD — select one CAC category (0 / 1–99 / 100–299 or ≥75th percentile / 300–999 / ≥1000 / incidental on nongated CT, mild or moderate–severe). ABI and carotid plaque (≥1.5 mm) are also entered here.
  7. Reading the results — ① pre-treatment and on-treatment risk (30-year risk shown only for age 30–59) ② lipid parameters & targets ③ PREVENT risk category ④ Statin Prescribing Recommendation (intensity, agents, decision basis). Risk-category-independent statin indications are flagged separately.
  8. Result Summary for EMR Copy — risk, indications, LDL target, treatment plan and NNT/NNH as bullet points.
  9. Stepwise add-on therapy when the LDL target is not met — ezetimibe → PCSK9 mAb / inclisiran → bempedoic acid. The order and expected LDL are shown for each pathway (very high risk, secondary prevention, diabetes, primary prevention, intolerance).
  10. NNT · NNH — statin NNT and adverse-effect NNH for this patient, with the Figure 4 benefit-harm comparison (NNT vs NND)
  11. Reference material — in Detailed mode with Hide explanations turned off, special situations (dialysis, cancer, transplantation, older adults), statin drug interactions, FAQ and references are available
4How the Recommendation Is Determined
  • Order of precedence: ASCVD (secondary prevention) → severe hypercholesterolemia (LDL ≥190 / non-HDL ≥220 / apoB ≥140) → diabetes → subclinical atherosclerosis (CAC etc.) → PREVENT risk category. If an earlier step applies, the decision is made regardless of the PREVENT score.
  • Category boundaries: PREVENT risk is rounded to the displayed precision (one decimal) before classification (e.g. 2.97% → 3.0% → borderline risk).
  • Exceptions at low risk (<3%): with 30-year ASCVD ≥10% (age 30–59) or LDL-C 160–189, starting a moderate-intensity statin is reasonable (COR 2a). In this case risk enhancers are not used in the prescribing decision.
  • Borderline/intermediate risk: risk enhancers support a shared decision toward starting a statin. If CAC has been measured, the CAC result takes precedence (CPR — Calculate → Personalize → Reclassify).
  • CAC = 0 + carotid plaque: treated as conflicting findings and shown as a shared decision (moderate-intensity statin vs lifestyle + reassessment).
  • Severe hypercholesterolemia: LDL <70 with confirmed HeFH, additional risk factors or subclinical atherosclerosis; otherwise <100. Assumes secondary causes have been excluded and values confirmed by repeat measurement.
  • Diabetes: age 40–75, moderate intensity regardless of LDL; high intensity if PREVENT ≥10% or multiple risk factors (≥2). Age 20–39, considered with PREVENT 10-yr ≥3%, 30-yr ≥10% or diabetes-specific risk enhancers.
  • Statin intolerance: the original indication is kept and only the therapy is replaced with nonstatins (ezetimibe, bempedoic acid, PCSK9 mAb).
  • Wording: when the basis is only COR 2a/2b, “considered” is used instead of “recommended”.
5Scope and Limitations
  • PREVENT applies to adults aged 30–79 without existing cardiovascular disease (primary prevention). Secondary prevention and LDL ≥190 are judged by criteria independent of PREVENT, and the NNT shown in these cases may underestimate the benefit.
  • ApoB is not a PREVENT input; it is used only for risk-enhancer detection and target assessment.
  • In end-stage kidney disease (eGFR <15 / dialysis level), newly starting a statin is not recommended (COR 3), and individual consultation is shown.
  • Pediatric criteria, HoFH management and LDL apheresis are not included in the calculation logic.
  • This tool supports clinical decision-making; the final prescribing decision rests on the clinician’s judgment of the individual patient.

3 keys to reading this table
  • Groups 1–4 are defined by "who the patient is" (whether the patient is a primary-prevention candidate, has LDL≥190, has diabetes, or already has ASCVD), Groups 5–6 are defined by "what testing showed" (whether plaque was seen on imaging, whether TG is high). So one patient can belong to several groups — e.g. if a patient with diabetes (Group 3) is incidentally found to have CAC 300 (Group 5), the stricter Group 5 target (<70) applies.
  • The PREVENT calculator (the risk tool used in Group 1) is not used at all for Groups 2 and 4. LDL ≥190 falls outside the calculator range (70–189), and a person who already has ASCVD "estimate future risk"— the answer is already known. Each of these two groups has its own separate criteria.
  • Group 5 splits internally — strictly speaking, the CAC pathway is not "completely independent of PREVENT"but rather "PREVENT decides whether to test, and once CAC is known that number is no longer used" — a two-step structure. CAC testing itself is recommended only at intermediate or select borderline risk (when the treatment decision is uncertain), so low-risk patients have no indication for CAC testing (except incidental findings on CT done for another reason, regardless of risk group). By contrast, the carotid-ultrasound plaque pathway keeps the PREVENT risk (intermediate or select borderline) as a standing prerequisite, so the PREVENT calculation is needed throughout.
  • Groups 3 (diabetes) and 6 (hypertriglyceridemia) are included in the PREVENT calculation as usual— diabetes is an input to the calculation, and TG is not a calculator eligibility criterion at all. These two groups have a "calculate as usual, with extra rules layered on top independent of the result" structure.
  • Secondary prevention (Group 4) is the only group that needs no risk re-estimation— having already had the disease is itself the answer, so a high-intensity statin is started unconditionally.
  • Within Group 5, CAC and carotid plaque have different levels of evidence. "risk-independent with established targets"— this character of Group 5 applies fully only to CAC. For carotid plaque, the 2026 ACC/AHA text itself states that "LLT is beneficial, but evidence is insufficient to set a specific LDL target", and the discussion is limited to intermediate or select borderline risk, so there is no basis for applying it to low-risk patients. Femoral plaque is not mentioned in this guideline at all.

The Cumulative LDL Burden Concept
The main determinant of ASCVD risk is not the LDL-C level itself but the duration × magnitude of LDL-C exposure— the integral (area under the curve).
Long-term exposure to varying LDL-C levels affects ASCVD outcomes more than seen in RCTs, owing to the duration of exposure.
It is therefore important to optimize healthy lifestyle habits from a young age and prevent the onset of dyslipidemia as early as possible.
2026 ACC/AHA Dyslipidemia Guideline — Section 4.1 (Lifestyle), Synopsis
Mendelian Randomization Evidence
People with genetically lifelong low LDL-C have low lifetime ASCVD risk. Conversely, high cholesterol in childhood persists into adulthood and increases midlife ASCVD risk.
Both short- to mid-term RCTs and long-term follow-up studies confirm that early detection and treatment lower LDL-C safely and effectively.
Section 3.2 Supportive Text #2; 4.2.8.1 Children and Adolescents
Key Cohort & Trial Evidence
CARDIA Cohort Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults
Even a modest increase in LDL-C area under the curve was associated with a significant increase in ASCVD events over 16 years of follow-up. Confirms the potential for early intervention to improve long-term event rates.
FHS-OS Cohort Framingham Heart Study Offspring
Cumulative exposure to hyperlipidemia in young adulthood dose-dependently increases.
FOURIER-OLE Open-Label Extension · evolocumab
The group on evolocumab from the start had a MACE rate lower → legacy effect of early treatment confirmed. Timing of treatment initiation affects outcomes.
CTT Meta-analysis
Each 1 mmol/L (38.7 mg/dL) LDL-C reduction gives about 22% relative risk reduction in ASCVD events. The lower the absolute LDL-C achieved, the more linearly the 5-year rate of major coronary events falls — baseline LDL 77 mg/dL or lower, further lowering still gives a 21% risk reduction.
Managing Cumulative LDL Burden by Life Stage (2026 ACC/AHA)
1 Childhood & Adolescence Section 4.2.8.1 · COR 1 B-NR
Lipid abnormalities occur in 1 in 5 adolescents; the FH phenotype in 1 in 250–300. Childhood cholesterol tracks into adulthood. Universal lipid screening recommended at age 9–11. Carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT) is detectable from age 8–10. Early detection → early lifestyle intervention is the foundation for reducing long-term risk.
2 Young Adulthood (age 18–39) Section 4.2.8.2 · COR 1 B-NR
Optimizing diet, physical activity and weight is recommended to reduce cumulative atherogenic lipid exposure and lifetime ASCVD risk (Class I). With 30-year risk ≥10% or LDL-C 160–189 mg/dL, a moderate-intensity statin is reasonable (Class 2a). Young ASCVD patients, even if below VHR criteria, have a long lifetime cumulative LDL exposure and a high recurrence risk.
3 Middle-Aged Adulthood (40–74) Section 4.2.3
Combine PREVENT 10-year/30-year risk assessment with risk-enhancing factors and CAC. Even if 10-year risk <3%, LLT may be considered to reduce cumulative exposure if atherogenic lipid burden is high. The 30-year risk estimate helps visualize long-term cumulative risk in younger middle-aged adults.
4 Older Adulthood (age ≥75) Section 4.2.8.3 · COR 1 C-EO
Age alone should not be the basis for discontinuing LLT. Continuing LLT is reasonable in patients ≥75 with life expectancy ≥2.5 years. An individualized approach accounting for already-accumulated atherosclerotic burden. Incorporate frailty, polypharmacy, functional status, and patient preference into shared decision-making.

After estimating 10-year ASCVD risk, when clinical uncertainty remains or the patient hesitates about the treatment decision, CAC measurement is useful for risk reclassification in borderline-to-intermediate risk. This approach was named the "CPR Framework(Calculate–Personalize–Reclassify)".

Beyond absolute 10-year/30-year risk, comorbidities, life expectancy, patient preference, and potential net benefit are incorporated into shared decision-making.

ApoB measurement is during LLT, especially in adults with ASCVD, CKM syndrome, TG ≥150, diabetes or on-treatment LDL-C <70 mg/dL (COR 2a).
Discordance between LDL-C and ApoB is common with elevated TG or metabolic syndrome. Using the Martin/Hopkins method to calculate LDL-C substantially reduces discordance compared with Friedewald, but ApoB still provides additional information.
ApoB can be measured in the non-fasting state and is standardized, making it practical.

ApoB — The Pathology of Particle Number
1
Every atherogenic lipoprotein (LDL, VLDL, IDL, Lp(a)) carries one ApoB-100 → ApoB = total number of atherogenic particles
2
Even with the same LDL-C, a higher particle count (ApoB) means higher risk — the core of the LDL discordance phenomenon
3
It is the particle itself that infiltrates and accumulates in the arterial wall → particle number is the causal driver of atherosclerosis
💡 At the same LDL-C of 100 mg/dL, many small particles (ApoB↑) are dangerous, while fewer large particles (normal ApoB) are relatively safe.

▸ What Is LDL/non-HDL Discordance?
Definition: LDL-C and non-HDL-C fall into different risk categories.
In other words, one marker is within target while the other exceeds it.

Cause: Because non-HDL-C = LDL-C + VLDL-C + IDL-C + Lp(a),
when VLDL remnants increase with TG↑, diabetes, insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome or CKD,
non-HDL-C can exceed target even when LDL-C is normal.

Target comparison:
 · Very high risk: LDL <55 mg/dL / non-HDL <85 mg/dL
 · High risk: LDL <70 mg/dL / non-HDL <100 mg/dL
 · Moderate: LDL <100 mg/dL / non-HDL <130 mg/dL

Clinical significance: If non-HDL-C exceeds its target even though the LDL-C target is met, this indicates
residual cardiovascular risk from remnant cholesterol (Remnant-C) such as VLDL remnants: residual risk.
In this case, confirm the actual atherogenic particle burden by measuring ApoB directly.

▸ "vulnerable to transporter inhibitors"— what it means Close ✕
It means that when co-administered with drugs that block hepatic transporters, blood levels of that statin rise substantially.
Background
Statins must enter hepatocytes to act. Before CYP metabolism, the hepatocyte membrane uptake transporter OATP1B1 draws circulating statin into the liver. Rosuvastatin is additionally affected by BCRP, an efflux transporter in the intestine and liver.
"Vulnerable"— what it means
Rosuvastatin and pitavastatin undergo minimal CYP metabolism but depend heavily on OATP1B1 for hepatic uptake. When a drug that blocks this entry point (e.g., cyclosporine, gemfibrozil) is added:
① The statin cannot enter the liver
② It remains in the circulation, increasing AUC several-fold (with cyclosporine: rosu ~7-fold, pita ~4.6-fold)
③ The higher circulating statin exposes muscle, increasing the risk of myopathy and rhabdomyolysis
④ Less drug reaches the liver, its site of action, so LDL lowering does not increase

9 Case — statin choice and LDL lowering with cyclosporine
▶
▸ Statin-by-statin assessment
Lipitor (atorvastatin): stop or switch. AUC up about 8.7-fold — avoid per US, ≤10 mg per EU. Even 10 mg gives exposure equivalent to 80 mg or more; splitting to half a tablet (5 mg) is off-label and, as an unscored film-coated tablet, gives variable dosing, so it is not advised.
Livalo (pitavastatin): Contraindicated (AUC ~4.6-fold, Cmax ~6.6-fold ↑).
Crestor (rosuvastatin): EU Contraindicated, US ≤5 mg (AUC about 7-fold, Cmax about 11-fold ↑).
Pravastatin ≤20 mg (start 10 mg) or fluvastatin ≤20 mg bid are the practical choices.
▸ LDL-lowering strategy
① Pravastatin 20 mg / fluvastatin — LDL about 30% ↓, sufficient for most primary prevention
② If the goal is not met, add ezetimibe — ~20% additional ↓. Cyclosporine raises ezetimibe exposure ~3.4-fold and ezetimibe may slightly raise cyclosporine levels → monitor cyclosporine levels and CK
③ For high-risk ASCVD or large gaps to goal, PCSK9 mAb (evolocumab, alirocumab) or inclisiran — no pharmacokinetic interactions
Drugs to avoid: fibrates (added nephrotoxicity and creatinine rise; myopathy risk with gemfibrozil) · bile acid sequestrants (lower cyclosporine absorption) · bempedoic acid (raises uric acid — cyclosporine also causes hyperuricemia and gout)
In practice: short-term use (e.g. several months for atopic dermatitis) + primary prevention → stop statin temporarily during use or substitute pravastatin 20 mg / long-term use or high risk → pravastatin + ezetimibe → PCSK9 inhibitor if insufficient. Cyclosporine itself raises LDL and TG, so reassess lipids during co-administration.

Frequently Asked Questions ▶
Q1. Is CoQ10 co-supplementation needed with statin therapy? ▶
Statins inhibit the mevalonate pathway, which reduces not only cholesterol but also its branch product CoQ10 (ubiquinone) — one proposed mechanism for statin-associated myopathy. However, guidelines do not recommend routine CoQ10 co-administration for several reasons.

① Inconsistent clinical evidence — RCTs and meta-analyses show mixed results; even where muscle-pain symptoms were reported to improve somewhat, evidence for improvement in CK (a marker of muscle injury) is even weaker. Most studies are small, short and rely on subjective scales (VAS), making it hard to exclude a placebo effect.
② The mechanism of myopathy is multifactorial — besides CoQ10 depletion, competing mechanisms include SLCO1B1 polymorphisms, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), and ryanodine receptor abnormalities, making standardization around a single supplement difficult.
③ Nocebo effect — in double-blind RCTs, the difference in myalgia rates between statin and placebo groups is much smaller than in observational studies, so the actual "true myopathy needing treatment" may itself be small in magnitude.
④ Priority of alternative strategies — checking CK, switching statins, dose adjustment, alternate-day dosing, and substitution with ezetimibe/PCSK9 inhibitors are more firmly evidence-based approaches recommended first.

Bottom line: the mechanism is plausible, but the quality and consistency of RCT evidence are insufficient, and as a supplement rather than a drug it does not clear the bar for an official recommendation. Given its low risk, this does not preclude individualized clinical use if a patient wishes to try it.
Q2. Very low LDL is fine — "cells that need it make it themselves"— is this claim valid? ▶
What is correct — almost all nucleated cells can synthesize their own cholesterol via the mevalonate pathway, and when intracellular cholesterol is low, SREBP-2 is activated, a compensatory mechanism that increases synthetic enzymes and LDL receptor expression together. This is supported by the observation that carriers of PCSK9 loss-of-function variants (LDL-C around 10–15 mg/dL) live normally without specific deficiencies, and FOURIER, ODYSSEY OUTCOMES and others showed no clear safety signal even at LDL-C in the 20s–30s.

What needs qualification
① Not all tissues are equally self-sufficient — the brain, protected by the blood-brain barrier, barely uses circulating LDL and instead runs its own astrocyte-ApoE-mediated local system, while steroidogenic tissues such as the adrenal cortex and gonads preferentially use LDL-receptor-mediated uptake (more energy-efficient during acute synthesis demand).
② "not needed"— not division of energy costs issue — de novo synthesis consumes much ATP and NADPH, so the LDL system in which the liver collects and distributes cholesterol is metabolically cheaper than every cell synthesizing from scratch.
③ Genetic deficiency and drug-induced very low LDL operate through different mechanisms — the steatorrhea, vitamin E deficiency, and neurological complications of abetalipoproteinemia (MTP deficiency) stem from failure of chylomicron formation itself, blocking absorption of fat-soluble substances altogether — fundamentally different from statins/PCSK9 inhibitors, which merely increase LDL particle clearance.

Summary: "cells that need it make it themselves"— the statement itself is physiologically correct, but it does not make the transport function of LDL unnecessary. On the other hand, counter-evidence that very low LDL is dangerous is also weak; current data mostly cover several years of follow-up, so a reservation is needed that very long-term (10+ years) safety is not yet settled.
Q3. Should statins be taken morning or evening? Does fasting vs. after-meal dosing matter? ▶
Timing — hepatic cholesterol synthesis (HMG-CoA reductase activity) peaks at night, so for short half-life statins (simvastatin, lovastatin, fluvastatin IR; half-life 2–3 hours), evening dosing is classically shown to give greater LDL-C lowering. By contrast, long half-life statins (atorvastatin ~14 h, rosuvastatin ~19 h, pitavastatin) showed no difference between morning and evening dosing in several RCTs, so timing does not matter.
Fasting vs fed — atorvastatin, rosuvastatin and pitavastatin can be taken without regard to food, but lovastatin (IR formulation) absorption depends on dietary fat, so taking it after meals is recommended.
Bottom line: with long half-life statins now dominant, the practical benefit of the "take it at night" rule is much smaller, and guidelines instead emphasize adherence — taking it at the same time every day without missing doses — as the most important variable.
Q4. Is alternate-day or 2–3x-weekly dosing effective instead of daily dosing? ▶
Used mainly for statin-intolerant patients who cannot take a statin daily, usually because of muscle symptoms. Long half-life, potent rosuvastatin and atorvastatin given every other day or 2–3 times a week lowered LDL-C by about 20–30% in small studies; the effect is smaller than daily dosing but "taking nothing at all"— clinically meaningful compared with that.
However, this is not a standard therapy in official guidelines but a salvage strategy in intolerant patients, and the evidence is limited to small RCTs and observational studies (Level B–C) with scarce cardiovascular outcome data. In practice, alternate-day dosing alone often fails to reach targets, so nonstatin combinations such as ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors or bempedoic acid are usually considered as well.
Q5. Why should grapefruit juice not be taken with statins? ▶
Furanocoumarins in grapefruit irreversibly inhibit intestinal CYP3A4. Statins metabolized by CYP3A4 (simvastatin, lovastatin, and partly atorvastatin) therefore reach much higher blood levels; with simvastatin, AUC increases of more than 10-fold have been reported with large amounts of grapefruit juice, raising the risk of myopathy and rhabdomyolysis.
In contrast, rosuvastatin (mainly CYP2C9, minimal metabolism), pravastatin (non-CYP pathway), and pitavastatin (minimal CYP metabolism) are relatively free of this interaction.
Clinical significance: occasional small amounts are not a major concern, but patients on CYP3A4-metabolized statins should be advised to avoid habitual, large-volume intake (roughly ≥1 L/day).
Q6. Is routine monitoring of liver enzymes (AST/ALT) needed during statin therapy, and at what threshold should statins be stopped? ▶
Routine monitoring — early-2000s guidelines recommended routine liver function testing, but large data sets later confirmed that serious statin hepatotoxicity (acute liver failure) is extremely rare, under 1 per million people per year. Since the 2013 ACC/AHA guideline, checking baseline ALT before treatment is recommended, but absent specific symptoms (jaundice, fatigue, right upper quadrant pain) or risk factors, routine repeat monitoring is no longer recommended. Mild asymptomatic ALT elevation (<3×ULN) is usually followed with repeat testing while continuing the statin, and many cases normalize spontaneously.

Discontinuation criteria — generally, ALT > 3×ULN persists, or symptoms such as jaundice, marked fatigue or right upper quadrant pain occur regardless of degree stopping is considered. If ALT is under 3×ULN and asymptomatic, the statin is usually continued with follow-up testing. For severity, Hy's law (ALT/AST >3×ULN + total bilirubin >2×ULN, normal ALP) is used as a reference.

Chronic liver disease (including compensated cirrhosis) is not an absolute contraindication; in patients with fatty liver disease (NAFLD/MASLD) in particular, the cardiovascular benefit is generally considered to outweigh hepatic risk, and current practice trends toward continuing or initiating statins (decompensated or acute liver failure remain contraindications).
Q7. When should statins be discontinued if CK (creatine kinase) rises? ▶
Baseline CK before treatment — not needed uniformly in every patient. It is a practical approach to check baseline CK only only when risk factors are present in those with risk factors such as older age (especially ≥75), reduced kidney function, uncontrolled hypothyroidism, personal or family history of muscle disease or statin intolerance, excessive alcohol use, interacting drugs (fibrates, especially gemfibrozil, certain antibiotics/antifungals, amiodarone etc.) or starting a high-intensity statin (per the 2018 ACC/AHA and NLA multisociety statements).

Asymptomatic CK elevation — a CK rise without muscle symptoms is not in itself a reason to stop the statin. Routine CK monitoring is not recommended either; after excluding other causes such as exercise or trauma, observation without symptoms is usual.

Stepwise approach when muscle symptoms are present
① CK < 4×ULN, mild and tolerable symptoms — continue the statin with symptom and CK follow-up.
② CK 4~10×ULN, with symptoms — consider a temporary hold or switching to a lower dose or another statin. CK is usually rechecked at 2-week intervals, and after normalization the patient is rechallenged with a lower dose, alternate-day dosing or a different statin.
③ CK > 10×ULN — discontinue immediately regardless of symptoms, and check renal function (Cr, eGFR) and urine myoglobin to evaluate for rhabdomyolysis; hydration is given as needed to prevent renal injury.
④ Rhabdomyolysis — usually defined as CK >10,000 U/L or accompanied by acute kidney injury, myalgia and dark urine (myoglobinuria); it is an emergency requiring immediate discontinuation, admission and aggressive fluids. Very rare (NNH about 8,000–12,500) but the most serious form of adverse effect.

After discontinuation — once CK normalizes, the usual strategy is to first rule out other causes (hypothyroidism, vitamin D deficiency, strenuous exercise, other myopathies), then rechallenge with a lower dose, alternate-day dosing, a hydrophilic statin (rosuvastatin, pravastatin), or pitavastatin; if this repeatedly fails, switch to non-statin therapy such as ezetimibe, a PCSK9 inhibitor, or bempedoic acid.
Q8. "statin intolerance": what are the exact definition and diagnostic criteria? ▶
Per the NLA (National Lipid Association) 2022 scientific statement: defined as one or more adverse effects associated with statin therapy that improve with dose reduction or discontinuation, divided into complete intolerance (no dose tolerated) and partial intolerance (the dose needed to reach the treatment target is not tolerated).
For diagnosis, at least 2 statins should be tried (one at the lowest approved dose) in principle; "took it once and had pain" alone does not establish intolerance. This definition is identical to that of 2026 ACC/AHA Figure 18.
While 5–30% of statin users report some form of intolerance, the proportion with genuine, pharmacologically confirmed intolerance on double-blind RCT testing is far lower, indicating that a substantial share reflects a nocebo effect.
Q9. If a patient develops myalgia on one statin, will switching to another statin cause the same problem? ▶
Not necessarily. Statins differ in lipophilicity/hydrophilicity, metabolic pathway, and muscle tissue penetration, so cross-reactivity is not 100%. Lipophilic statins (simvastatin, atorvastatin, lovastatin) penetrate the muscle cell membrane more readily and are reported to have relatively higher rates of muscle symptoms, while hydrophilic statins (rosuvastatin, pravastatin) tend to have lower rates.
The typical clinical approach is, in order: ① rechallenge with a lower dose of the same statin, ② switch to a hydrophilic statin or one with a different metabolic pathway (e.g., pitavastatin), ③ change from daily to alternate-day dosing — and many patients succeed in tolerating a statin through one of these methods. In a subset, however, a true class effect recurs with every statin, in which case non-statin therapy (ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, bempedoic acid) is substituted.
Q10. What is the evidence level behind the claim that statins cause cognitive decline or dementia? ▶
Controversy grew after the FDA added to statin labels in 2012 the "memory loss/confusion" possibility, but this was based on postmarketing adverse-event reports (case reports), not data proving causation.
Subsequent large RCT meta-analyses (CTT data, JUPITER subgroup analyses, etc.) and observational studies have not found consistent evidence that statins increase cognitive decline or dementia risk; some observational studies even reported a mild protective association (interpretation requires caution given possible reverse causation). Society statements since 2018 (ACC/AHA and others) maintain that current evidence does not support a causal relationship.
Symptoms reported in clinical practice are generally reversible (improving within a few weeks of discontinuation), and the mechanistic rationale for a long-term degenerative effect is considered weak.
Q11. Is LDL-C "lower is better"— or is there a lower limit? ▶
In large RCTs and Mendelian randomization studies, cardiovascular benefit continues to rise linearly even when LDL-C is lowered to the 20s mg/dL, and no clear "no benefit below this level" threshold has been identified — this underpins the current "lower is better, and longer is better" paradigm. PCSK9 inhibitor RCTs such as FOURIER and ODYSSEY OUTCOMES also found no significant safety signal (hemorrhagic stroke, neurocognition) down to LDL-C 20–30 mg/dL.
However, there are reservations about asserting a complete "no lower limit". ① Most follow-up is several years long, so long-term (10+ years) data at very low LDL are limited. ② PCSK9 LOF cohorts reflect a lifelong adapted congenital state, not exactly the same as rapid drug-induced lowering. ③ Some observational studies reported associations of very low LDL-C with hemorrhagic stroke and certain cancers, but these are mostly interpreted as LDL lowering caused by underlying disease (reverse causation) rather than causation.
Current guidelines (ESC/EAS 2019) set graded LDL-C targets by risk group, very high risk <55 mg/dL, high risk <70 mg/dL (each with ≥50% reduction from baseline), but "lower than this is dangerous"— no explicit lower limit of this kind is set.
Q12. What is the evidence that statins are first-line therapy in hypertriglyceridemia? Are there large studies? ▶
There is no large RCT testing the cardiovascular effect of statins only in patients with hypertriglyceridemia. The evidence is indirect but consistent in three directions.

① Subgroup and post hoc analyses of large statin RCTs — in a 4S post hoc analysis (Ballantyne, Circulation 2001), the group with elevated LDL-C plus high TG and 'lipid triad' had about a 50% reduction in major coronary events, larger than the group with elevated LDL-C alone. In the CTT meta-analyses (Lancet 2005, 2010), the relative risk reduction per 1 mmol/L LDL-C was consistent across baseline subgroups, so the statin effect does not disappear with high TG. Primary-prevention benefit was also shown in diabetic dyslipidemia populations where high TG is common (CARDS etc.).
② Mechanistic evidence (apoB-centered view) — statins remove not only LDL but also VLDL remnants, lowering non-HDL-C and apoB. The higher the baseline TG, the larger the TG reduction, roughly 10–30%. Mendelian randomization (Ference, JAMA 2019) showed that the cardiovascular benefit of TG lowering is proportional to the reduction in apoB, supporting the argument that the number of atherogenic particles, rather than the TG level, should be lowered.
③ Failure of TG-lowering strategies alone — fibrate RCTs (FIELD, ACCORD-Lipid) were neutral overall, with a benefit signal only in the high-TG/low-HDL subgroup. PROMINENT (pemafibrate, NEJM 2022) lowered TG about 26% without reducing cardiovascular events, and apoB actually rose slightly. Among omega-3s, REDUCE-IT (icosapent ethyl) showed benefit in high-TG patients already on statin therapy , whereas STRENGTH (EPA+DHA) was neutral. Omega-3 is therefore positioned as an add-on on top of statins.

Summary: the case for statins in high TG rests on three pillars — consistent benefit in statin RCT subgroups, the mechanism of lowering apoB and remnants, and the counter-evidence that strategies lowering TG itself failed to reduce events. The absence of a dedicated high-TG RCT must be acknowledged as a limitation.
Q13. In a patient for whom a high-intensity statin is recommended, may treatment start at moderate intensity and be titrated up? If the target is reached on moderate intensity, may it be continued? ▶
① Starting low and titrating up — supported by the guideline text
2026 ACC/AHA Section 4.2.1.1 (Statins) states that some populations, particularly East Asians, are prone to adverse effects because of genetic differences in drug metabolism, and that initial therapy should start at a lower dose. The same section describes that, along with the expected % reduction, the absolute LDL-C actually achieved is also an objective treatment goal, and that the actual reduction varies by population and adherence. Therefore, starting East Asian patients on moderate intensity (e.g. rosuvastatin 5–10 mg, atorvastatin 10–20 mg) and titrating after a lipid check at 4–12 weeks does not conflict with the guideline.

② Continuing moderate intensity once the target is reached — not directly addressed in the guideline (interpretation)
The basic direction of the guideline is "to target"— not "up to the maximally tolerated dose", not just to the target. Recommendation 2 for LDL-C ≥190 calls for a maximally tolerated statin, citing greater risk reduction with high vs moderate intensity, and Recommendations 3–4 add nonstatins when the target is not met on a maximally tolerated statin. The CAC, ASCVD and high-risk diabetes pathways require "≥50% reduction and target" together. Not treating the target as a stopping point — "lower is better"— is the basic philosophy of the guideline.

③ Statin intensity is defined by expected % reduction, not by drug name
Table 6 defines high intensity as ≥50%, moderate as 30–49% and low as <30% reduction. For example, from LDL-C 210 mg/dL, reaching <100 requires about 53% or more; with an average response (30–49%), moderate intensity would leave LDL at 107–147 mg/dL. A patient who reaches <100 on moderate intensity has shown an individual high-intensity-level response (≥50%), effectively meeting the high-intensity goal. In this case, continuing moderate intensity for tolerability is "maximally tolerated" and "reaching target"— a reasonable choice that satisfies both.
Conversely, if the target is met but the reduction is under 50% (e.g. LDL-C 150 → 95, a 37% reduction), in pathways requiring high intensity or ≥50% reduction, titrating up within tolerance is closer to the intent of the guideline.

⚠ The prescribing recommendation and the NNT figure may differ
This NNT is calculated using only the PREVENT 2023 10-year risk score × CTT RRR, so it may diverge from the prescribing recommendation above.
· LDL-C ≥190 mg/dL — an absolute indication outside the PREVENT range (LDL 70–189); a high-intensity statin is recommended even if PREVENT risk is low, so the NNT may come out high
· Diabetes, CKD stage 3–4 — a factor already in the PREVENT equations, so the NNT is calculated correctly. However, guideline LLT recommendations apply to age 40–75, and because the guideline follows a risk-independent prescribing pathway, the recommended intensity and the NNT may seem to differ in clinical meaning
· With risk enhancers (RE) — REs are not part of the PREVENT inputs, so any RE-driven increase in recommended intensity is not reflected in the NNT calculation (see the adjusted NNT*)
· Secondary prevention — baseline risk is assumed as a fixed value (~25–40%), so individuals vary
· Adjusted NNT* — applies only the single strongest-evidence factor among CAC, Lp(a), and RE (in that priority order: CAC > Lp(a) > RE). Overlapping multiple factors risks over-adjustment due to violated independence assumptions
The 2026 ACC/AHA guideline states that NNT-based discussion is borderline–intermediate risk (3–10%) primary prevention most useful in this range.

① Adults (>18 years) — start at diagnosis (2026 ACC/AHA 4.2.4.3, e1198–1199)
• Because the target population is defined only as "adults with severe hypercholesterolemia", there is no lower or upper age limit.
• there is no required preceding period of lifestyle modification — after excluding secondary causes per Recommendation 1 (COR 1), start a maximally tolerated statin per Recommendation 2 (COR 1, B-R).
• independent of PREVENT risk. It applies even when 10-year risk is 1%, as at age 34.
• In practice, it is reasonable to start after confirming the values with repeat measurement.

② Children and adolescents — from age 8, after lifestyle modification (4.2.8.1 Recommendation 2, e1216, COR 1, B-R)
• Population: age ≥8, LDL-C persistently ≥160 mg/dL, clinical picture consistent with FH
• Condition: 3–6 months of lifestyle modification— if the response is insufficient, start a statin and add other LLT as needed.
• The threshold is not the adult value (190) but 160. About 1 in 250–300 children and adolescents has LDL-C ≥160 consistent with HeFH.
• Target: relative to pre-treatment, ≥50% reduction and LDL-C ≤130 mg/dL is generally recommended (e1218).
• Pediatric approved ages (e1217 table): age ≥8 — rosuvastatin 5 and 10 mg, pitavastatin, pravastatin / age ≥10 — atorvastatin, simvastatin

③ Homozygous FH (HoFH) — immediately at diagnosis, including infancy (e1216 footnote, see 4.2.4.4)
• Aggressive LLT is needed from diagnosis, and infancy is no exception.

Why start early — cumulative LDL-C exposure
• In Mendelian randomization studies, lifelong low LDL-C is associated with low lifetime ASCVD incidence.
• High cholesterol in childhood persists (tracks) into adulthood, and statins slow subclinical atherosclerosis progression in pediatric HeFH.
• Key evidence: Dutch 20-year follow-up of pediatric HeFH (Luirink, NEJM 2019) — HeFH patients who started statins in childhood had fewer cardiovascular events by age 30 than their untreated parents.
